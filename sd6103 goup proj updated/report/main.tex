\begin{abstract}
This project develops a normalized PostgreSQL database linking HDB resale
transactions to nearby hawker centres. The reproducible pipeline combines 2023--2025
resale records, current HDB coordinates, official hawker attributes, and supplied hawker
coordinates. After validation and matching, the database-ready instance contains 26 towns,
9,385 HDB blocks, 78,670 transactions, 129 hawker centres, and 40,354 proximity links.
We define three great-circle-distance bands and provide six analytical SQL queries plus
candidate indexes. The complete schema, processed data, and all six analytical queries
were executed successfully in PostgreSQL 18 through pgAdmin. The final all-table query
returned 154 groups, matching the independent CSV cross-check.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Public housing and hawker centres are important parts of Singapore's social infrastructure.
The project asks whether housing attributes, remaining lease, town, and accessibility to
affordable food are associated with resale prices. We use official HDB transaction data
\citep{datagov_resale}, official market and hawker attributes \citep{datagov_hawker}, and
the geolocated HDB dataset described by \citet{lan2022site}. The implementation follows
relational normalization and integrity principles \citep{elmasri2016fundamentals}.

\section{Task 1: Conceptual and Logical Design}
\subsection{Proximity definition}
We use haversine great-circle distance between the latitude/longitude of an HDB block and
a hawker centre. The three mutually exclusive bands are: \emph{near}, $[0,500)$ m;
\emph{medium}, $[500,1000)$ m; and \emph{far}, $[1000,2000]$ m. Links beyond 2 km are
not stored because they do not represent useful local access. The exact distance is stored;
its band is derived from a reference relation, avoiding two stored facts that can disagree.
This choice is reproducible, requires no proprietary
routing API, and is consistent with the coordinate datasets, although it approximates
walking accessibility.

\subsection{Conceptual ER model}
Figure~\ref{fig:er} follows the min--max convention taught in the
SD6103 lectures \citep{ntu2026er}: rectangles are entity types, diamonds are relationship types, ellipses
are attributes, underlining denotes keys, and a dashed ellipse denotes a derived
attribute. The final model follows the assignment terminology \textbf{HDB\_FLAT}, while
defining its data-supported grain explicitly: one geocoded block--street address, not an
individual dwelling unit. Thus \texttt{flat\_id} identifies a block-address record.
Introducing a second unit-level entity would invent identities absent from the sources.
Flat type, storey range, floor area, model, lease and price instead describe a resale
transaction observation.

A town may contain zero or many blocks, while every block belongs to exactly one town.
A flat record may have zero or many resale transactions, while every transaction belongs
to exactly one flat record through \emph{has\_sale}. The \emph{nearby} relationship is
M:N. Its \texttt{distance\_m} is a
relationship attribute and \texttt{proximity\_class} is derived from that distance.

The seven proposed diagrams contain three recurring alternatives. Table~\ref{tab:ermchoices}
records the final decisions so that conceptual notation is not mixed with implementation
details.

\subsection{Relational schema and normalization}
\begin{longtable}{p{0.25\textwidth}p{0.67\textwidth}}
\toprule

\midrule
\endhead

HDBFlat & \textbf{flat\_id} (PK), town\_id (FK, NOT NULL), block, street\_name,
postal\_code, latitude, longitude; UK(block, street\_name) \\
ResaleTransaction & \textbf{transaction\_id} (PK), flat\_id (FK, NOT NULL), sale\_month,
flat\_type, storey\_range, floor\_area\_sqm, flat\_model,
lease\_commence\_year, remaining\_lease\_months, resale\_price, source\_row\_key (UK) \\

distance\_m; relation produced by the M:N mapping \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
The 1:N \emph{contains} and \emph{has\_sale} relationships are mapped by NOT NULL foreign
keys on their N-sides. Following the lecture mapping rule for M:N relationships
\citep{ntu2026relational},
\texttt{nearby} contains both participant keys as a composite primary key
and the relationship attribute \texttt{distance\_m}. \texttt{ProximityLevel} is introduced
at logical design time as a reference relation. The view
\texttt{nearby\_classified} derives the matching level rather than storing a
redundant \texttt{proximity\_level\_id} in every pair.

For transactional correctness, the database makes the source-row hash unique, rejects
non-positive prices and areas, constrains coordinates and lease values, cascades deletion
only for derived flat--hawker links, and retains the default restrictive behavior for
transaction history. PostgreSQL range exclusion prevents overlapping proximity-level
definitions. During preparation, \texttt{has\_fresh\_market} is set from the official
market-stall count; it remains \texttt{NULL} when no official match exists, preserving
the distinction between ``no market'' and ``not matched''.

Every non-key attribute describes its relation's key, the whole key, and no other
non-key attribute. Town names, proximity definitions, and hawker facts are separated,
eliminating update anomalies; the schema is in 3NF and, under the declared candidate keys,
BCNF. Domain checks constrain Singapore coordinates, positive areas/prices, monthly dates,
postal-code format, lease bounds, and distance bounds. Complete executable DDL appears in
Appendix~\ref{app:ddl}.

\section{Task 2: Data Acquisition and Population}
\subsection{Sources and reproducibility}
The download script retrieves the specified GitHub \texttt{hdb.csv} and the official HDB resale source,
then retains transactions from 1 January 2023 through 31 December 2025,
and the official hawker attribute table. The supplied
\texttt{hawker\_geo.csv} provides hawker coordinates. The transformation program writes
one CSV per database table and computes proximity links before PostgreSQL bulk loading.

\subsection{Transformations}
Text keys are trimmed and upper-cased; sale months are converted to first-of-month dates;
numeric fields are parsed into typed values; and source rows receive SHA-256 keys for
idempotent loading. The early resale file lacks a remaining-lease field, so remaining
months are computed as
\[
  99\times12 - \bigl(12(y_s-y_l)+(m_s-1)\bigr),
\]
where $(y_s,m_s)$ is the sale month and $y_l$ is the lease commencement year. Values are
bounded to $[0,1188]$. Hawker attributes are joined to coordinates by six-digit postcode,
which is more reliable than names containing abbreviations. The official market-produce
stall count is retained, and PostgreSQL generates the fresh-market flag from it. Haversine
distance is computed for every candidate block--hawker pair; links within 2 km are stored,
while their bands are assigned only through the classification view.

\subsection{Loading issues and resolutions}
All 78,670 transactions in the specified 2023--2025 interval match a geocoded block;
no in-scope transaction is excluded. Of 129 geolocated hawkers, 104 match official attributes by postcode;
81 have market-produce stalls, 23 do not, and 25 remain unknown. Seventeen geolocation rows
have blank addresses, represented as SQL \texttt{NULL}. PostgreSQL staging tables and
foreign-key joins enforce load order; \texttt{ON CONFLICT} makes repeat runs safe.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
Straight-line distance ignores street networks, crossings, and public transport. Current
HDB coordinates are a current reference snapshot rather than historical coordinates, and hawker matching leaves
25 unknown market statuses. Prices are nominal rather than inflation-adjusted. Despite
these limitations, the deliverable provides a normalized, constraint-rich, operational
PostgreSQL backend and readable SQL for all required questions. Schema creation, five-file
bulk loading, integrity checks, indexing, six analytical queries, and the controlled
performance experiment have all been verified in PostgreSQL 18.
